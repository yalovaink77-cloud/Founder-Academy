# Problem Validation — Research Synthesis

## Purpose

Problem validation determines whether a real, important, and commercially meaningful customer problem exists before significant product development begins.

Validation is not an attempt to prove that the founder's idea is correct.

It is a process for testing assumptions and collecting evidence strong enough to support one of three decisions:

- Continue
- Revise
- Stop

## Core Distinction

Problem validation, solution validation, and Product-Market Fit are different stages.

### Problem Validation

Confirms that a specific group of people experiences a meaningful problem.

### Solution Validation

Confirms that the proposed solution improves the customer's current situation.

### Product-Market Fit

Confirms that the delivered product creates enough continuing value for customers to adopt, retain, recommend, or pay for it.

These stages must not be treated as interchangeable.

## Founder Academy Standard

A problem is not validated because:

- people say the idea is interesting,
- friends provide positive feedback,
- competitors exist,
- a survey receives positive responses,
- a landing page receives visits,
- users join a waiting list,
- or an AI model predicts demand.

These are signals, not proof.

A problem becomes increasingly credible when multiple independent forms of evidence point in the same direction.

## Evidence Hierarchy

Validation evidence should be evaluated by strength.

### Level 1 — Opinions

Examples:

- “That sounds useful.”
- “I would probably use it.”
- Survey answers
- Social media reactions
- AI-generated customer simulations

These provide weak evidence because no meaningful commitment is required.

### Level 2 — Observed Past Behaviour

Examples:

- The customer describes a recent instance of the problem.
- The customer already uses a workaround.
- The customer spends time solving it manually.
- The customer has previously searched for a solution.
- The customer has tried competing products.

Past behaviour is stronger than future promises.

### Level 3 — Commitment

Examples:

- Providing contact information
- Joining a structured beta
- Scheduling a follow-up
- Sharing private workflow details
- Introducing the founder to another potential customer
- Investing time in testing a prototype

Commitment provides stronger evidence because the customer gives up something valuable.

### Level 4 — Economic Evidence

Examples:

- Deposit
- Pre-order
- Paid pilot
- Signed letter of intent
- Purchase order
- Subscription
- Completed purchase

Real payment is generally the strongest early evidence that the problem and proposed value matter.

## Customer Discovery

Customer Discovery is a founder responsibility.

Its purpose is to test hypotheses and understand customer problems directly from prospective customers.

It should not be outsourced before the founder has developed a deep understanding of:

- the customer,
- the problem,
- the current workflow,
- the existing alternatives,
- the buying process,
- and the conditions required for adoption.

The founder must personally hear where assumptions are wrong.

## Interview Principle

The goal of an interview is learning, not selling.

Do not begin by explaining the product.

Begin with the customer's life and behaviour.

Useful questions include:

- Tell me about the last time this happened.
- What triggered the problem?
- What did you do?
- Which tools or services did you use?
- What was difficult?
- How much time or money did it cost?
- Who else was involved?
- What have you already tried?
- Why did the existing solution fail?
- What happened when the problem was not solved?
- Who decides whether a solution is purchased?

Avoid questions such as:

- Would you use this?
- Do you like this idea?
- Would you pay for this?
- Is this a good product?
- Which features should we build?

These questions invite speculation, politeness, and invented future behaviour.

## Problem Severity

A problem becomes more commercially attractive when several conditions are present:

- It happens frequently.
- It creates meaningful emotional, financial, operational, or safety consequences.
- The customer is already trying to solve it.
- Existing alternatives are expensive, difficult, fragmented, or inadequate.
- The person experiencing the problem can be reached.
- A buyer exists.
- The buyer has the authority and ability to pay.
- The problem is important enough to change behaviour.

A large market with weak pain may be less valuable than a smaller market with urgent pain.

## Validation Experiments

Different assumptions require different experiments.

### Interviews

Best for understanding problems, workflows, language, alternatives, and buying behaviour.

### Observation

Best for discovering differences between what customers say and what they actually do.

### Prototype Test

Best for testing comprehension, usability, and whether a proposed workflow creates value.

### Concierge Test

The founder manually delivers the intended outcome before automating the product.

Best for learning the real workflow and determining whether the outcome matters.

### Wizard of Oz Test

The customer sees a product-like experience while important operations are performed manually behind the scenes.

Best for testing demand before building expensive automation.

### Landing Page Test

Presents a clear promise to a defined audience and measures meaningful actions.

Traffic alone is not validation. Stronger actions include:

- qualified signups,
- demo requests,
- beta applications,
- deposits,
- or purchases.

### Painted Door Test

Displays a feature or offer before it exists and measures whether users attempt to access it.

The test must not deceive users about availability. The unavailable status should be explained after the action.

### Pre-Sale or Paid Pilot

Tests whether customers will make an economic commitment before full development.

This is one of the strongest early validation methods.

## Experiment Design

Every validation experiment must define:

- Assumption
- Target customer
- Test method
- Success threshold
- Failure threshold
- Time limit
- Cost limit
- Evidence collected
- Decision rule

Example:

Assumption:

Small piercing studios lose meaningful time manually answering repeated aftercare questions.

Test:

Interview 15 studio owners and ask about their most recent week of customer follow-up.

Success threshold:

At least 8 describe the problem without prompting, at least 5 use a recurring workaround, and at least 3 agree to test a proposed solution.

Decision:

Continue, revise the segment, or stop.

Thresholds must be established before results are known.

## Multiple Evidence Rule

No important business assumption should be accepted from one experiment alone.

Use multiple evidence types where practical:

1. Interview evidence
2. Behavioural evidence
3. Commitment evidence
4. Economic evidence

Confidence rises when different tests support the same conclusion.

## B2C and B2B Differences

### B2C

The user and buyer are often the same person.

Validation may focus on:

- emotional urgency,
- frequency,
- convenience,
- habits,
- retention,
- willingness to subscribe,
- and acquisition cost.

### B2B

The user, buyer, decision-maker, and budget owner may be different people.

Validation must identify:

- who experiences the problem,
- who approves the purchase,
- who controls the budget,
- how procurement works,
- how long the buying cycle takes,
- and what measurable business outcome justifies payment.

Positive feedback from a user does not prove that an organisation will purchase.

## False Validation

Common false-positive signals include:

- compliments,
- likes,
- followers,
- unqualified waiting-list signups,
- downloads without activation,
- free users who never return,
- interviews conducted only with friends,
- survey questions that reveal the desired answer,
- showing the solution before understanding the problem,
- and changing success criteria after seeing results.

Validation must be designed to disprove assumptions, not protect them.

## AI Limitation

AI can assist with:

- organizing research,
- identifying interview themes,
- generating hypotheses,
- summarizing transcripts,
- designing experiments,
- and finding contradictions.

AI cannot replace evidence from real customers.

Synthetic personas and simulated interviews may help prepare research, but they do not validate demand, behaviour, willingness to switch, or willingness to pay.

## Decision Framework

### Continue

Continue when strong, repeated evidence shows:

- a specific customer exists,
- the problem is meaningful,
- current alternatives are inadequate,
- customers demonstrate commitment,
- and the proposed direction deserves further testing.

### Revise

Revise when:

- the problem exists but affects a different segment,
- the pain is real but the proposed solution is weak,
- the user and buyer are different,
- willingness to pay is lower than expected,
- or the initial distribution assumption is wrong.

### Stop

Stop when:

- the problem rarely occurs,
- customers do not attempt to solve it,
- consequences are insignificant,
- no reachable buyer exists,
- repeated experiments fail,
- or stronger opportunities deserve the resources.

Stopping is not failure.

It is successful risk reduction.

## Completion Criteria

Problem Validation is complete enough to proceed when:

- A specific initial customer segment is defined.
- The problem is described using customer language.
- Recent examples of the problem are documented.
- Current alternatives and workarounds are known.
- Frequency and consequences are understood.
- The user, buyer, and decision-maker are identified.
- Multiple experiments support the same conclusion.
- At least one meaningful commitment has been observed.
- Remaining assumptions are documented.
- A clear Continue, Revise, or Stop decision is recorded.

## Founder Academy Principle

Validation does not eliminate uncertainty.

It prevents the founder from investing heavily while the most dangerous assumptions remain untested.
