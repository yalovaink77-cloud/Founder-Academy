# Positioning and Pricing Sprint

## Purpose

Define why the right customer should choose the product and establish a pricing hypothesis that reflects value, positioning, and business sustainability.

## Sprint Questions

> Why should this customer choose us instead of the alternatives?

> What value exchange should we test?

## Inputs

- Customer research.
- Problem evidence.
- Product/value hypothesis.
- Known alternatives.
- Existing pricing evidence, if any.

## Part A — Positioning

### Step 1 — Define the Target Context

State:

- Who the product is for.
- Which situation makes the product relevant.
- Which problem or desired outcome matters.

Avoid broad claims such as “for everyone.”

### Step 2 — Identify Real Alternatives

Alternatives may include:

- Direct competitors.
- Manual workflows.
- General-purpose tools.
- Doing nothing.
- Hiring a person or service.
- Existing habits.

The customer compares against these alternatives, not only category competitors.

### Step 3 — Identify Differentiated Value

For each important difference ask:

- Does the customer care?
- Is it believable?
- Can it be demonstrated?
- Does it create a meaningful outcome?

A feature is not automatically a differentiator.

### Step 4 — Build the Positioning Statement

Capture:

- Target customer.
- Relevant situation/problem.
- Main alternative.
- Distinct value.
- Proof/reason to believe.

### Step 5 — Build Message Hierarchy

Define:

1. Primary promise.
2. Main supporting benefits.
3. Proof points.
4. Objection responses.
5. Call to action.

Use language grounded in customer research.

## Part B — Pricing

### Step 6 — Define the Pricing Objective

Clarify what pricing must accomplish now.

Examples:

- Test willingness to pay.
- Support premium positioning.
- Reduce adoption friction.
- Establish sustainable unit economics.
- Learn which package creates the clearest value exchange.

### Step 7 — Identify Value and Reference Points

Consider:

- Customer outcome/value.
- Current alternative cost.
- Time or risk saved.
- Competitor/reference prices.
- Frequency of use.
- Buyer budget and purchase process.
- Business economics.

Competitor price is evidence, not an automatic answer.

### Step 8 — Create a Pricing Hypothesis

Specify:

- Who pays.
- What they pay for.
- Price/price range.
- Billing or transaction model.
- Included value.
- Important limits or tiers.
- Why the hypothesis makes sense.

### Step 9 — Define the Test

Possible evidence:

- Real purchase.
- Paid pilot.
- Preorder where appropriate.
- Offer comparison.
- Sales conversation.
- Checkout conversion.
- Upgrade behavior.

Avoid treating “I would pay” as equivalent to payment behavior.

## Founder Decision

Choose:

- Adopt current positioning/pricing hypothesis for testing.
- Narrow the audience.
- Change the value/message.
- Change pricing structure.
- Return to customer/product research.

## Required Outputs

- Target context.
- Alternative map.
- Positioning statement.
- Message hierarchy.
- Proof points.
- Pricing objective.
- Pricing hypothesis.
- Pricing test.
- Founder decision.

## Exit Criteria

The founder can clearly answer:

- For whom is this product?
- In what situation?
- What alternative is it replacing?
- Why is it meaningfully different?
- What evidence supports the claim?
- What will customers be asked to pay?
- How will willingness to pay be tested?

## AI Role

AI may research alternatives, synthesize customer language, generate positioning options, compare pricing structures, and design tests.

Founder owns the market position, value promise, pricing risk, and final decision.
